The Problem

Landers crash because their software wasn't built for the real world

The final 60 seconds of descent are the most dangerous minutes of any lunar mission. Two of the last four commercial landers didn't survive them.

👁

Sensors go blind

Dust and solar glare blind optical cameras at the exact moment accurate perception matters most — powered descent over hazardous terrain.

🗺

Maps don't match reality

Orbital imagery lacks the resolution for real-time hazard avoidance. Landers routinely arrive at terrain that looks nothing like what they were programmed to expect.

⚙️

No safe fallback

When sensors disagree or perception fails, classical guidance either aborts or flies blind. There is no system that gracefully handles uncertainty and stays safe.

💥  2 of the last 4 commercial lunar landers crashed on descent — both attributed to navigation and sensor failures, not structural issues.
What We Build

The missing integration layer

Every existing player solves one piece. We close the loop between all four — and license it as a single, certifiable product.

01 — Perception

See through degradation

Crater-aware optical navigation that continues functioning under lens flare, dust, and extreme lighting. Outputs a confidence score per detection — not just a binary result — so downstream systems know exactly how much to trust it.

02 — Uncertainty

Know what you don't know

Propagates "how wrong could this position fix be" forward in time. Eliminates the silent failure mode where a degraded perception estimate is treated as ground truth, eliminating the source of most descent accidents.

03 — Arbitration

Decide who to trust

When sensors give conflicting readings, the system decides which estimate to rely on — dynamically, in real time, without a ground operator. Can coordinate across multiple assets: lander, orbiter relay, or secondary surface robot.

04 — Safety Filter

Guarantee safety, not just performance

A control-barrier-function layer that mathematically guarantees the final trajectory never violates a hazard constraint — even if everything upstream was wrong. This is certifiable safety, not statistical confidence. The layer mission assurance reviewers have been waiting for.
